Sinking land in Semarang: who pays for it?

Land in north and east Semarang is sinking, yet neither the city's tax records nor the province's asset accounts show any loss of value. This study set out to explain that gap.

Status: work in progress. Fieldwork took place on 28–30 October 2026, and the findings below are preliminary and not for citation.

Semarang is where a loss of land value should show first

Kota Semarang is one of 11 districts in Java where part of the ground sinks faster than 5 cm a year. In 38 of its 177 villages, the ground sank at that rate in 2017–2023.

Property tax is also a large part of the city's own revenue. PBB-P2, the land and building tax, brought in Rp628 billion in 2024. If coastal land loses value, the city budget should be among the first places to show it.

Fast subsidence runs from east Semarang into Demak

In Kota Semarang, 38 of 177 villages sink faster than 5 cm a year, and in neighbouring Demak, 70 of 249 do. To the west the picture changes: only 6 of Kendal's 286 villages sink that fast. About 405,000 buildings in Semarang and Demak stand on ground sinking faster than 5 cm a year

In Kota Semarang, 189,114 of 836,000 buildings stand on ground that sinks faster than 5 cm a year, or 23 percent. In Demak the share is higher, 34 percent, or 215,689 buildings. Together their roofs cover about 3,900 hectares.

Most of Demak's remaining buildings sink more slowly, between 1 and 5 cm a year. In Kota Semarang, two thirds stand on ground that barely moves.

This measures exposure, not loss. A building on sinking ground has not necessarily lost value, and the count does not separate houses from factories.

In Sayung, next to Semarang, the sea already covers 44 percent of seven coastal villages

Sayung, in Demak, borders Kota Semarang to the east. In its seven coastal villages, 1,479 of the 3,358 hectares inside village boundaries are no longer mapped as land. In Sidogemah the share is 75 percent, and in Purwosari, Sriwulan, and Bedono it is between 64 and 70 percent.

Sayung stopped building; Genuk kept going

Between 2000 and 2024, Sayung lost 383 hectares of settlement and gained only 34. Genuk, just across the border in Kota Semarang, lost 42 hectares and built 82.

Both sink fast, and Genuk's coastal villages have lost land to the sea as well. The difference is whether people keep building. Most of Sayung's loss came before 2014; in 2014–2022 the two figures were closer, 114 lost against 88 built.

A descriptive comparison from MapBiomas land cover, used to frame field questions. It does not explain why the two areas differ.

Land under the sea still sits inside village boundaries, and often on land certificates and asset registers. Who carries that loss is the question this study asks.

The study asks who bears the loss when coastal land sinks

The study asks whether land and buildings on the sinking coast have lost value, and who bears the loss. Owners, banks, and local government are the three candidates. Each would see the loss in a different record: sale prices, collateral values, or tax and asset accounts.

The desk analysis found exposure, not loss

I first combined the public data into one record for each of the 177 villages, joining radar estimates of subsidence from Ohenhen et al. (2026) to village boundaries, registered population, building footprints, night-time light, PODES flood reports, and regional finance records.

Villages that sink fast have not lost people. Population change tracks distance from the old city centre far more closely than the rate of subsidence, and villages near the centre lost residents whether or not their ground was sinking. Property tax revenue also kept growing.

Residents follow distance from the centre, not the sinking ground

Between 2021 and 2025, 99 of Kota Semarang's 177 villages lost registered residents. Of the 87 villages within 5 km of Simpang Lima, the old centre, 80 lost people, whether their ground sank or not. Villages farther out mostly grew, including 14 on ground sinking faster than 5 cm a year in Genuk and Pedurungan.

Property tax kept growing in Semarang but stalled in Demak

In 2018 prices, Kota Semarang collected 30 percent more land and building tax (PBB-P2) in 2024 than in 2018, and Kendal more than twice as much. Demak's receipts have stayed near Rp70 billion a year since 2019, so their real value has fallen for five years.

That flat line is not proof that subsidence shrank Demak's tax base. Rates and assessed values may simply not have been updated. It is one of the questions the field visit put to local revenue agencies.

But exposure is not the same as loss, and the public data stop there. Land values by zone, tax arrears by property, and transaction records are held by local agencies.

Fieldwork suggests the loss is paid for each year instead of written down

The field visit was designed to ask for those records and to test what the data suggested. The team met four planning, finance, and revenue agencies of Kota Semarang and Central Java province.

Officials described flooded offices, abandoned houses, and government land that has turned into fish ponds while still being listed as land. Yet assets stay on the books at their recorded value, and tax assessments have not been lowered anywhere on the coast. The cost shows up instead as land fill, raised roads, pumps, and sea walls, paid by households, firms, and the government.

The study can now ask a sharper question: not only whether coastal assets have lost value, but who is paying to keep that value on paper, and how much.

Radar estimates match ground stations to within 0.3 cm a year

Every subsidence figure on this page comes from radar estimates by Ohenhen et al. (2026). Before using them, I compared them with GNSS stations run by BIG, Indonesia's mapping agency. At 57 stations with an official velocity, the radar median within 500 metres differs from the station by a median of 0.28 cm a year, and by less than 0.64 cm at nine stations in ten.

But most stations stand on stable ground, so the test is weakest where it matters most. Only two stations sink fast. In Pekalongan, GNSS records 12.2 cm a year and the radar 9.2. In Genuk, Semarang, the figures are 8.4 and 7.6, from a record of only two years. Both confirm fast sinking, and the radar may understate the fastest rates.

Next steps and limits

The answer needs property-level tax records and the province's register of regional assets, which the team has requested.

The work is not finished. Interview notes are still being transcribed and checked. The agencies have not yet shared the requested records, so none of the field findings can be measured yet. Officials are not named, and the agencies are described only by function.

My role

I built the village dataset and the land subsidence dashboard behind the desk analysis, and I drafted the report's analysis of Kota Semarang.
