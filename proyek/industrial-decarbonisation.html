Factory energy records break in 6 of 16 years, so the analysis compares sectors within each year

This stream, with WRI Indonesia, prepares the evidence for a green industrial policy package to bring manufacturing to net zero by 2050, starting from plant-level BPS data on nine energy-intensive subsectors, 2000–2015.

Status: work in progress. The 2015 figures come from a first round of data cleaning that the team has not yet settled, so they are preliminary and not for citation.

Summary

Indonesia aims for net zero emissions by 2060, and the target for manufacturing is ten years earlier. Meeting it depends on a few heavy industries: cement, steel, fertiliser, chemicals, and paper among them. This stream, carried out with WRI Indonesia, studies which policies could cut their energy use and emissions without stalling growth.

The evidence starts from the BPS survey of large and medium manufacturers, which records energy use plant by plant. Before drawing any chart, the team checked those records year by year, and 6 of the 16 years show breaks. In 2010, electricity use at the same plants fell 39 percent, and in 2014 it rose 86 percent, while their workforce did not change.

Those years are breaks in the records, not in production. So the dashboard compares sectors and regions within a year and does not show energy use as a trend.

Within one year the data still say a lot. In 2015, 78 percent of the 12,813 plants in the nine subsectors were in Java. For the median plant in eight subsectors, energy cost between 2.3 and 5.3 percent of the value of output. Plants in Java spent nearly half of their energy budget on PLN grid electricity, while plants outside Java spent the largest share on diesel.

The stream follows nine energy-intensive subsectors

The nine subsectors are those in the government's roadmap for industrial decarbonisation.

MaterialsCement; iron and steel; glass and ceramics; pulp and paper
ChemicalsFertiliser; other chemicals
Consumer goodsFood and beverages; textiles; automotive

Each subsector is defined by its KBLI industry codes. The dashboard uses whole code divisions, which is broader than the roadmap's product list, and shows both definitions side by side.

Electricity at the same plants fell 39 percent in 2010 and rose 86 percent in 2014, while employment stayed flat

Each year, the check matches every plant to its own record from the year before, between 14,700 and 22,700 plants a year. Electricity bought from PLN by those same plants fell 39 percent in 2010 and rose 86 percent in 2014. Their median employment did not change in any year.

Real factories do not lose a third of their power use without losing workers. The six red years break in three ways. In 2004, 2010, and 2014, electricity at the same plants jumped or fell while employment stayed flat. In 2006, the number of plants surveyed rose 42 percent, a sign that the survey frame changed. In 2001 and 2002, industry codes were cut short or most fuel types went unrecorded.

Prices point the same way. Dividing what plants paid by the kWh they reported gives an electricity price three to five times PLN's official price in 2010–2015, so the reported quantities are too low even where spending looks plausible.

So the six red years can be used only to compare sectors within that year, and a trend is read only across the green years. For the same reason, the 2015 charts below use spending in rupiah, not physical energy.

Four in five plants in the nine subsectors are in Java

In 2015 the survey counted 12,813 plants in the nine subsectors, and 10,011 of them were in Java. Food and beverages and textiles make up nearly three-quarters of the count. The heaviest industries are few and spread out: 16 of the 22 cement plants are outside Java. West Java alone holds 42 percent of the textile plants and 60 percent of the glass and ceramics plants.

Energy costs the median plant 2–5 percent of output in most subsectors, and 21 percent in glass and ceramics

For the median plant in eight of the nine subsectors, fuel and electricity cost between 2.3 and 5.3 percent of the value of output in 2015. Glass and ceramics stands apart at 21.4 percent, and a tenth of its plants spend about a third.

That figure needs care. About seven in ten plants in the group make roof tiles and bricks in small kilns, not the flat glass and floor tiles in the government's roadmap. Cement has only 22 plants, so its spread is wide.

Energy cost is spending on fuel and electricity divided by the value of output, per plant. Box = middle half of plants; white line = median; thin line = 10th to 90th percentile. Extreme values with no repeated entries are set aside. Source: BPS IBS 2015; preliminary cleaning by KOMITMEN.

Factories in Java run mostly on the grid, and those outside Java on diesel

In 2015, plants in six of the subsectors spent Rp83.4 trillion on energy, Rp70.7 trillion of it in Java. PLN electricity took 47 percent of spending in Java and diesel 9 percent. Outside Java the order reverses: diesel 38 percent and PLN 20 percent. In Jambi diesel takes 55 percent, and plants in Riau generate much of their own power.

So a cleaner grid would reach most of the factory energy bill in Java but much less of it elsewhere, where replacing diesel matters more.

Share of energy spending in rupiah; Rp trillion in brackets. Cement, fertiliser, and automotive are left out because their provincial energy figures are not yet reliable. Source: BPS IBS 2015; preliminary cleaning by KOMITMEN.

West Java has 43 of Indonesia's 143 industrial estates

The dashboard also maps where industry clusters. West Java has 43 industrial estates with 21,400 hectares, 31 of them in Bekasi, Karawang, and Purwakarta along the toll road east of Jakarta. Twenty-eight of them had started by 2015, the last year of the plant data.

Its only special economic zone, Lido in Bogor, was set up in 2021 for tourism, not manufacturing. Industry in West Java grew in estates, not in zones.

Next steps and limits

The next step is to agree on how to clean the plant records, and then to estimate energy intensity by subsector and region for the policy package.

The limits are serious. The survey covers plants with 20 or more workers. Energy is partly recorded as spending, so prices and quantities can be confused. The data end in 2015, so they describe the starting point of the transition, not its progress.

My role

I extended the analysis from three years to 16, 2000–2015, and built the data-quality report that flags each year. I also checked the subsector totals against BPS publications and built the industry dashboard, from which the charts on this page are drawn.

The stream is led by senior researchers at KOMITMEN and run with WRI Indonesia. A colleague wrote the first analysis of 2013–2015 that this work builds on.
