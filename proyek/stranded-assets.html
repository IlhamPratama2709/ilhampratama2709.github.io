Summary

An asset is stranded when it loses its value before the end of its planned life. In Indonesia, that can happen to a coal mine when demand for coal falls, to a palm oil mill when its market closes, or to a house when the ground beneath it sinks into the sea.

The stream maps where these assets are and who holds them. Nearly all of the 521 coal mining sites in the data are in Kalimantan and Sumatra, yet half of the country's coal power capacity is in Java. On the coast, 3.35 million people in 11 districts of Java live on ground sinking faster than 5 cm a year.

Exposure is not loss. Whether any of these assets has lost value, and who bears the loss, is the question the team is now testing, starting with fieldwork in Semarang.

Coal and palm oil face transition risk; coastal land faces physical risk

The three assets lose value for different reasons. Coal and palm oil are exposed to transition risk: demand, prices, and rules can change faster than a mine or a mill can recover its cost. Coastal land is exposed to physical risk, because the ground sinks and the sea comes in whatever the market does.

CoalMines, coal power plants, and the captive plants that feed nickel smelters and other industry. Value falls if coal power is retired early or export demand weakens.
Palm oilMills and the plantations that supply them. Value falls if buyers or trade rules shut out oil from cleared forest or peat.
Coastal landHouses, factories, and public land on sinking ground. Value falls as floods and the sea reach land that is still on certificates and asset registers.

For each asset the stream asks the same question: who would carry the loss? Owners would see it in prices, banks in the value of their collateral, and local governments in tax receipts and asset accounts.

Coal is mined in Kalimantan and Sumatra but burned mostly in Java and in nickel provinces

Of 521 coal mining sites, 99 percent are in Kalimantan and Sumatra. East Kalimantan alone has 188, and South Kalimantan 121.

The coal power plants sit elsewhere. Java holds 51 percent of the 62.2 GW of coal capacity, led by Banten with 11.2 GW and Central Java with 10.2 GW. Sulawesi and North Maluku hold another 29 percent, nine tenths of it in captive plants that supply nickel smelters and other industry.

Palm oil is different: 96 percent of the 1,227 mills are in Sumatra and Kalimantan, close to the plantations. If coal or palm oil assets lose value, the loss would be spread across provinces that mine, process, and burn, and each would feel it through a different budget.

Two areas hold 94 percent of the people on fast-sinking ground in Java

Ground sinking faster than 5 cm a year appears in only 11 of Java's 119 districts. But 3.35 million people live on it.

The Bandung basin, inland, accounts for 47 percent of them, with 929,000 people in Kota Bandung and 645,000 in Kab. Bandung. The north coast of Central Java accounts for another 47 percent, from Demak and Kota Semarang to Pekalongan and Brebes. In Kota Pekalongan, 22 of 27 villages sink that fast, and 288,000 of its 322,000 residents live in them.

The two areas carry different risks. In Bandung the ground sinks under a city far from the sea. On the north coast it sinks beside the sea, so land can be lost to it, as in Sayung, Demak.

About 405,000 buildings in Semarang and Demak stand on ground sinking faster than 5 cm a year, yet tax records and asset accounts show no loss of value. Next steps and limits

Every figure on this page measures exposure. None of them shows that an asset has lost value or says how much.

The next step is to find records that would show a loss: land values by zone and tax arrears by property for coastal land, and asset accounts for public land. For coal and palm oil, the stream will link assets to their owners and lenders, so a fall in value can be traced to the balance sheet that carries it.

The data have limits of their own. The mining sites come from satellite-based and public inventories and may miss small or unlicensed mines. The radar estimates may understate the fastest sinking, and they cover only villages with enough measurement points.

My role

I built the village dataset and the land subsidence dashboard, which join radar estimates to village boundaries, population, night-time light, flood reports, and land cover for 24,689 villages in Java. The district figures on this page come from that dataset.

The dashboard of coal and palm oil assets was started by a colleague. I reworked it and added the captive power plants and poverty data. I also drafted the analysis of Kota Semarang for the case study.

The stream is led by senior researchers at KOMITMEN, and the framing above reflects the team's discussions.
