B40 biodiesel saves about 40 million tonnes of CO2 a year, and clearing 62,500 hectares of peat forest would cancel it

Two topics from my internships at Bank Indonesia, rebuilt from public sources: what a higher biodiesel blend costs in land and peat, and how Indonesia's rules on natural-resource export proceeds compare with five other Asian economies.

Summary

Indonesia has raised the share of palm-based biodiesel in its diesel from 20 percent in 2016 to 40 percent in 2025, and B50 has applied since 1 July 2026. The Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources (ESDM) puts the emission saving of B40 at about 41 million tonnes of CO2e a year. The saving is real only if the palm oil does not come from newly cleared peat.

The calculator below shows how thin that margin is. At B40 the biodiesel needs about 14.8 million tonnes of crude palm oil, the output of some 3.7 million hectares of oil palm. Clearing 62,500 hectares of peat swamp forest, under 2 percent of that area, releases as much carbon as B40 saves in a year.

The second half of the page traces the rules on export proceeds from natural resources (devisa hasil ekspor sumber daya alam, DHE SDA). Since 2019 Indonesia has moved from requiring that the proceeds enter the domestic financial system to requiring that non-oil-and-gas exporters keep all of them at home for a year. None of the five neighbours compared here asks for that much.

Move the slider to change the blend. B40 means 40 percent biodiesel and 60 percent fossil diesel. Every figure scales in a straight line with the blend.

The first three figures trace the palm oil back to the land. At B40 the program needs 14.3 million kilolitres of biodiesel a year, or about 12.6 million tonnes. Making it takes 14.0–14.8 million tonnes of crude palm oil, depending on the conversion yield, and growing that oil takes 3.5–3.7 million hectares.

The last two figures turn the saving into peat. Clearing a hectare of peat swamp forest for oil palm releases about 640 tonnes of CO2 from vegetation alone. A drained hectare then keeps emitting about 100 tonnes a year as the peat decomposes. So 62,500 hectares of cleared forest wipe out one year of B40's saving, and 400,000 hectares of drained peat wipe it out every year.

The land figure is an equivalent, not a forecast of new plantations. Most of the oil would be diverted from exports grown on existing land. But each step up in the blend raises demand for palm oil, and the peat figures show how little expansion onto peat it takes to erase the gain.

The 640 tonnes leaves out the peat itself, so it understates what clearing releases once the peat burns or decomposes. The 100-tonne rate sits well above the IPCC default because it includes the fast losses of the first years after drainage. The calculator shows the two separately and does not add them. Biodiesel volumes in kilolitres are converted to tonnes with the density above; skipping that step, and treating a kilolitre as a tonne, overstates the land by about 14 percent.

Indonesia now asks natural-resource exporters to keep all non-oil-and-gas proceeds at home for a year

Indonesia's exports of coal, palm oil, metals, timber, and fish earn a large share of its foreign currency, but much of it used to stay in accounts abroad. Since 2019 four government regulations (peraturan pemerintah, PP) have set, and then tightened, what exporters must do with those earnings.

  1. PP 1/2019 · in force 10 January 2019

    Proceeds must enter the Indonesian financial system

    Exporters of mining, plantation, forestry, and fishery products had to bring their proceeds into a special account (rekening khusus DHE SDA) at a domestic foreign-exchange bank by the end of the third month after export. They could withdraw the money at once. Sanctions ranged from fines to suspension of exports and of the business licence.

  2. PP 36/2023 · in force 1 August 2023

    30 percent must stay for at least three months

    For export declarations worth USD 250,000 or more, at least 30 percent of the proceeds had to be retained for at least three months. Exporters could hold them in the special account, in bank deposits, in instruments issued by the export credit agency LPEI, or in Bank Indonesia instruments such as its foreign-currency term deposit for export proceeds. The sanction became suspension of export services.

  3. PP 8/2025 · in force 1 March 2025

    Non-oil-and-gas exporters must retain 100 percent for 12 months

    Mining other than oil and gas, plantations, forestry, and fisheries must now retain all of their proceeds for at least 12 months. Oil and gas stays at 30 percent for three months. Funds placed in bank, LPEI, or Bank Indonesia instruments cannot be withdrawn before maturity. A new Article 11A lets exporters spend from the special account on five things, and the amount spent reduces what they must retain:

    1. conversion to rupiah at the same bank;
    2. taxes, non-tax state revenue, and other payments to the government in foreign currency;
    3. dividends in foreign currency;
    4. raw materials, auxiliary materials, and capital goods that are unavailable or insufficient in Indonesia;
    5. repayment of foreign-currency loans taken to buy capital goods.

  4. PP 21/2026 · in force 1 June 2026

    Retention moves to state-owned banks, and conversion is capped

    The shares and periods are unchanged. According to statements by the Coordinating Minister for Economic Affairs, retained proceeds must now be placed in state-owned banks (Himbara), and no more than half may be converted to rupiah. I have not yet checked these changes against the text of the regulation.

Sources: PP 1/2019, PP 36/2023, and PP 8/2025 as published in the State Gazette (texts read through the DDTC legal database); PP 21/2026 from ANTARA, 5 May 2026 and Kontan, 21 May 2026; Bank Indonesia instruments under PBI 7/2023.

Indonesia is the only one of six economies to require exporters to keep proceeds for a fixed period

Every economy here wants export earnings to pass through its own banks, but only Indonesia tells exporters how long to keep them there. Malaysia and India set a deadline for bringing proceeds home. Thailand asks only for large receipts to come home, and since January 2026 only above USD 10 million a transaction. The Philippines asks for nothing beyond reporting.

India goes the other way on retention. Exporters may hold earnings in a foreign-currency account, but what they have not used by the end of the following month must be converted to rupees. The rules also serve different ends. Thailand relaxed its rules to let money flow out and ease pressure on the baht, while Indonesia tightened its own to keep foreign currency at home.

My role

I interned at Bank Indonesia in two units. From August to October 2025 I was in the Department of Reporting Management and Compliance, working on an issue note on natural-resource export proceeds and foreign exchange reserves. I processed data for the note and compared the rules on export proceeds in six economies. The table above is a fresh version of that comparison, checked again against public sources.

From November 2025 to February 2026 I was at the Bank Indonesia Institute, which was studying a move to B50 biodiesel and beyond. I worked on the trade-off between emission savings and the land and peat that palm oil needs, which the calculator above rebuilds from public parameters. I also wrote my own internship report on a business model for fishers' cooperatives under the Koperasi Desa Merah Putih program.

The issue note and the biodiesel study are internal Bank Indonesia documents, and their data and findings are not shown here.
