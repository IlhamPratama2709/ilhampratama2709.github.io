B40 biodiesel saves about 40 million tonnes of CO2 a year, and clearing 62,500 hectares of peat forest would cancel it

From my internship at the Bank Indonesia Institute: what a higher biodiesel blend costs in land, and how little peat it takes to cancel the saving, rebuilt as a calculator from public parameters.

Summary

Indonesia has raised the share of palm-based biodiesel in its diesel from 20 percent in 2016 to 40 percent in 2025, and B50 has applied since 1 July 2026. The Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources (ESDM) puts the emission saving of B40 at about 41 million tonnes of CO2e a year. The saving is real only if the palm oil does not come from newly cleared peat.

The calculator below shows how thin that margin is. At B40 the biodiesel needs about 14.8 million tonnes of crude palm oil, the output of some 3.7 million hectares of oil palm. Clearing 62,500 hectares of peat swamp forest, under 2 percent of that area, releases as much carbon as B40 saves in a year.

Move the slider to change the blend. B40 means 40 percent biodiesel and 60 percent fossil diesel. Every figure scales in a straight line with the blend.

The first three figures trace the palm oil back to the land. At B40 the program needs 14.3 million kilolitres of biodiesel a year, or about 12.6 million tonnes. Making it takes 14.0–14.8 million tonnes of crude palm oil, depending on the conversion yield, and growing that oil takes 3.5–3.7 million hectares.

The last two figures turn the saving into peat. Clearing a hectare of peat swamp forest for oil palm releases about 640 tonnes of CO2 from vegetation alone. A drained hectare then keeps emitting about 100 tonnes a year as the peat decomposes. So 62,500 hectares of cleared forest wipe out one year of B40's saving, and 400,000 hectares of drained peat wipe it out every year.

The land figure is an equivalent, not a forecast of new plantations. Most of the oil would be diverted from exports grown on existing land. But each step up in the blend raises demand for palm oil, and the peat figures show how little expansion onto peat it takes to erase the gain.

The 640 tonnes leaves out the peat itself, so it understates what clearing releases once the peat burns or decomposes. The 100-tonne rate sits well above the IPCC default because it includes the fast losses of the first years after drainage. The calculator shows the two separately and does not add them. Biodiesel volumes in kilolitres are converted to tonnes with the density above; skipping that step, and treating a kilolitre as a tonne, overstates the land by about 14 percent.

My role

From November 2025 to February 2026 I interned at the Bank Indonesia Institute, which was studying a move to B50 biodiesel and beyond. I worked on the trade-off between emission savings and the land and peat that palm oil needs, which the calculator above rebuilds from public parameters. I also wrote my own internship report on a business model for fishers' cooperatives under the Koperasi Desa Merah Putih program.

The biodiesel study is an internal Bank Indonesia document, and its data and findings are not shown here. Before this placement I worked on rules for export proceeds in another department.
