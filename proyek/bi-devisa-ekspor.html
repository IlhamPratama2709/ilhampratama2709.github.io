Indonesia is the only one of six Asian economies that makes exporters keep their proceeds at home for a fixed period

From my internship in Bank Indonesia's Department of Reporting Management and Compliance: how the rules on natural-resource export proceeds (DHE SDA) have changed since 2019, and how they compare with Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand, India, and China.

Summary

Coal, palm oil, metals, timber, and fish earn a large share of Indonesia's foreign currency, but for years much of it stayed in accounts abroad. Since 2019 the government has moved from requiring that these export proceeds enter the domestic financial system to requiring that non-oil-and-gas exporters keep all of them at home for a year.

None of the five neighbours compared here asks for that much. Malaysia and India set deadlines for bringing proceeds home but do not say how long they must stay. Thailand and the Philippines ask for little beyond reporting, and Thailand loosened its rule again in January 2026 to ease pressure on the baht.

Indonesia now asks natural-resource exporters to keep all non-oil-and-gas proceeds at home for a year

Since 2019 four government regulations (peraturan pemerintah, PP) have set, and then tightened, what exporters of mining, plantation, forestry, and fishery products must do with their earnings.

  1. PP 1/2019 · in force 10 January 2019

    Proceeds must enter the Indonesian financial system

    Exporters of mining, plantation, forestry, and fishery products had to bring their proceeds into a special account (rekening khusus DHE SDA) at a domestic foreign-exchange bank by the end of the third month after export. They could withdraw the money at once. Sanctions ranged from fines to suspension of exports and of the business licence.

  2. PP 36/2023 · in force 1 August 2023

    30 percent must stay for at least three months

    For export declarations worth USD 250,000 or more, at least 30 percent of the proceeds had to be retained for at least three months. Exporters could hold them in the special account, in bank deposits, in instruments issued by the export credit agency LPEI, or in Bank Indonesia instruments such as its foreign-currency term deposit for export proceeds. The sanction became suspension of export services.

  3. PP 8/2025 · in force 1 March 2025

    Non-oil-and-gas exporters must retain 100 percent for 12 months

    Mining other than oil and gas, plantations, forestry, and fisheries must now retain all of their proceeds for at least 12 months. Oil and gas stays at 30 percent for three months. Funds placed in bank, LPEI, or Bank Indonesia instruments cannot be withdrawn before maturity. A new Article 11A lets exporters spend from the special account on five things, and the amount spent reduces what they must retain:

    1. conversion to rupiah at the same bank;
    2. taxes, non-tax state revenue, and other payments to the government in foreign currency;
    3. dividends in foreign currency;
    4. raw materials, auxiliary materials, and capital goods that are unavailable or insufficient in Indonesia;
    5. repayment of foreign-currency loans taken to buy capital goods.

  4. PP 21/2026 · in force 1 June 2026

    Retention moves to state-owned banks, and conversion is capped

    The shares and periods are unchanged. According to statements by the Coordinating Minister for Economic Affairs, retained proceeds must now be placed in state-owned banks (Himbara), and no more than half may be converted to rupiah. I have not yet checked these changes against the text of the regulation.

Sources: PP 1/2019, PP 36/2023, and PP 8/2025 as published in the State Gazette (texts read through the DDTC legal database); PP 21/2026 from ANTARA, 5 May 2026 and Kontan, 21 May 2026; Bank Indonesia instruments under PBI 7/2023.

Malaysia and India set deadlines for bringing proceeds home; Thailand and the Philippines ask for little

Every economy here wants export earnings to pass through its own banks, but only Indonesia tells exporters how long to keep them there. Malaysia and India set a deadline for bringing proceeds home. Thailand asks only for large receipts to come home, and since January 2026 only above USD 10 million a transaction. The Philippines asks for nothing beyond reporting.

India goes the other way on retention. Exporters may hold earnings in a foreign-currency account, but what they have not used by the end of the following month must be converted to rupees. The rules also serve different ends. Thailand relaxed its rules to let money flow out and ease pressure on the baht, while Indonesia tightened its own to keep foreign currency at home.

My role

From August to October 2025 I interned in the Department of Reporting Management and Compliance, working on an issue note on natural-resource export proceeds and foreign exchange reserves. I processed data for the note and compared the rules on export proceeds in six economies. The table above is a fresh version of that comparison, checked again against public sources.

The issue note is an internal Bank Indonesia document, and its data and findings are not shown here. After this placement I moved to the Bank Indonesia Institute to work on biodiesel and peat.
