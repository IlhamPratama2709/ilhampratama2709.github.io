Paving the Way, Sustaining Hope: The Impact of the Trans-Java Toll Road on East Java's Economy

The toll road made transport firms far more productive with fewer workers, and it added lower-productivity jobs in food and lodging.

Executive summary

The Trans-Java toll road changed East Java's transport sector more than its wider economy. Across 30 districts from 2011 to 2023, transport GDP within 15 km of a toll gate rose by about 10 percent after opening, and output per transport worker rose by about 56 percent. The number of transport workers fell.

Accommodation and food moved the other way. Employment rose near toll gates by 0.197 log points and productivity fell by about 16 percent, which suggests that new jobs were added at low value per worker. GDP in that sector did not change significantly.

Manufacturing shows no significant change in GDP, jobs, or productivity. Total GDP looks higher by about 5 percent, but districts near the gates were already on a different path, so that figure cannot be read as the effect of the road.

A toll road helps in specific places and sectors. Its gains would reach further with better feeder roads, stronger skills, and spatial planning that is aligned with each new exit.

How the study works

  1. Toll sections openEast Java sections opened in stages from 2017 to 2020
  2. Nearby districts are identifiedDistrict centres within 15 km of a toll gate
  3. Three sectors are followedTransport, manufacturing, and accommodation and food
  4. Districts are compared30 districts, against those more than 15 km from a gate

The question

Infrastructure is widely seen as a driver of growth, yet the gains from a large road are often uneven. Most studies report an average for a whole region, which can hide which places and sectors benefit.

This paper asks how the Trans-Java toll road affected GDP, employment, and labour productivity in East Java, and whether those effects differ by distance and by sector.

Data and method

East Java has 38 districts, and the paper keeps 30. It leaves out four that already had toll access, Gresik, Pasuruan, Sidoarjo, and Surabaya, and four on Madura, which sit outside the road's reach.

Distance is measured in QGIS from each district's administrative centre to the nearest toll gate. Districts within 15 km count as treated once the road opens. The paper uses difference-in-differences with staggered adoption and district fixed effects, then tests parallel trends and repeats the transport results with cut-offs from 5 km to 25 km.

Findings

Three sectors, three outcomes

Transport

GDP rises by about 10 percent and productivity by about 56 percent, while employment falls by 0.344 log points. Firms appear to move more with fewer workers.

Accommodation and food

Employment rises by 0.197 log points, GDP does not change significantly, and productivity falls by about 16 percent.

Manufacturing

No significant change in any of the three outcomes. A nearby toll gate did not bring a visible shift in factory activity.

The transport result holds at other distances

Changing the cut-off from 5 km to 25 km leaves the pattern in place. Transport productivity ranges from 0.359 to 0.487 and employment from −0.282 to −0.416, and both stay significant. Transport GDP stays positive at most cut-offs, from 0.065 to 0.098.

Interpretation

Lower travel time and cost let transport firms carry more with fewer people. They may be using larger vehicles, more capital, or better logistics, though district data cannot show which.

Food and lodging may be growing through small roadside businesses near the gates, which add jobs without much value per worker. Low-productivity firms may also be moving into newly reached areas. The paper treats both as plausible explanations.

Policy implications

  1. Improve last-mile links. Better feeder roads would connect industrial areas and farm centres to the gates, since the gains concentrate near them.
  2. Pair roads with skills and technology. Vocational training in logistics, hospitality, and precision manufacturing would raise the value of jobs along the corridor.
  3. Plan each exit with local land use. Rest areas could showcase local firms, and new gates should follow spatial plans and industrial estate plans.

Limitations

District data can hide effects that concentrate in one or two subdistricts near an exit. Unobserved factors, such as local governance and spatial planning, may also affect both the road's route and the results. Distance is measured in a straight line, so it does not capture the condition of feeder roads, and travel time may be longer in southern East Java. Total GDP and total productivity fail the parallel-trends test.
